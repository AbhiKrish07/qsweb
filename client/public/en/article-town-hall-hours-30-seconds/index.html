Picture a resident of your town. She wants to know whether the civil registry office is open on Saturday morning. She types the town hall’s name into her phone, lands on the town’s website, searches for two minutes, finds nothing and gives up. She’ll call on Monday, or make the trip for nothing.

Multiply that by a few hundred residents a month. You end up with an overloaded switchboard, staff who keep answering the same questions, and people who leave feeling that their local government doesn’t make their lives any easier.

All that for an opening time.

Your town hall’s website has become its front desk

Before they meet a staff member, most residents first come across a website. It sets the tone. If it’s slow, confusing or visibly neglected, the message is clear even if nobody puts it into words: around here, nobody pays much attention to you.

The reverse works too. A site where people find what they’re looking for in thirty seconds has a discreet but real effect on their relationship with the institution. It doesn’t make headlines. It builds trust.

The three most common pitfalls of local government websites.

The showcase site disguised as a public service

It recounts the local government’s work, highlights elected officials, publishes council decisions. Fine. But it doesn’t answer the concrete questions people have, and communicating has never been the same thing as providing a service.

RGAA accessibility treated as a formality

The RGAA, France’s general accessibility improvement framework, is a legal requirement for the public sector. Not an optional extra. Beyond the rules, there’s a reality: some of your residents browse with a screen reader, impaired vision or simply an old phone. An inaccessible website excludes citizens.

The site built for fiber

A homepage loaded with heavy visuals and autoplay videos performs very well at the office. Much less so in a town in the interior of French Guiana, on a patchy network, with a limited data plan. Lean digital design isn’t just an environmental issue. It’s first and foremost a question of equal access.

The real difficulty

A public website has to meet six requirements at once: strategy, design and technology, lean digital design, accessibility, performance and data security. None of them can be sacrificed to make things look nice.

Striking that balance is the hard part. Not going live.

Quiet Studio, official partner of ACCD’OM

We now champion this conviction alongside ACCD’OM, the only association devoted exclusively to defending the interests of all local governments in the French overseas territories. More than 130 members, 11 territories, three oceans, and more than 80% of the overseas population represented.

It all started with a meeting.

“We met the members of the ACCD’OM board at the 2025 Salon des maires in Paris. A partnership felt like the obvious next step: we share the same vision, supporting and structuring the growth of our territories.”

Terry Geoffroy, Business Development Director and partner at Quiet Studio

Our role as official partner plays out on two levels. We support the association in its strategic communications decisions. And we strengthen its impact with member local governments through training in artificial intelligence and communications, because building the skills of elected officials and staff is worth more than any turnkey service.

On top of that comes a concrete commitment: a 15% discount, with no time limit, for all ACCD’OM member local governments on our website and digital platform design services.
